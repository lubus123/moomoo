More plants,

The US is absorbing its largest-ever wave of dairy processing capacity — ~$8–11 billion across 50+ plants. Textbook logic says more buyers competing for milk should lift farm prices. Instead, USDA forecasts the all-milk price down ~10% in 2026. This deck explains why the paradox is real, dissects the Upper-Midwest negative-basis symptom, and lands on how to re-wire your S&D model.

The mechanism

Milk price is set by the product, not by the buyers

Under Federal Milk Marketing Orders, farm milk uses end-product pricing: the mailbox price is a formula run backwards from wholesale cheese, butter, whey and powder. So causation flows product → milk. New capacity floods the product market, wholesale prices fall, and the milk price is dragged down with them.

The "more buyers → higher price" intuition is a raw-milk scarcity story. It only holds if milk is short and price is set by local bidding. Neither is true here: milk supply responded, and the FMMO formula ties the announced price to national commodity values. Local competition still exists — it shows up as basis (premiums over the class price), not in the class price itself.

Why the formula bites

The Class III formula — and a permanent re-rating

Class III milk value is literally computed from the cheese price, minus a regulated make allowance, times yield, plus whey value. When cheese falls, Class III falls one-for-one. In June 2025 the make allowances were raised — a permanent downward shift in how much of the product price reaches the farm.

Class III ≈ (Cheese − make allowance) × yield  +  whey value

Why it matters for your model

Higher make allowances (up ~5–7¢) mechanically lower every classified milk value at a given product price. If mailbox prices lag while Class III sinks, basis risk widens for DRP / LGM-Dairy hedges. Treat June-2025 as a structural level shift — rebase your milk-from-product bridge, don't treat it as noise.

05 / 10

The timing trap

Capacity is lumpy & fast; milk is slow

A cheese plant switches on in a quarter; a milk supply response takes years — heifer inventory is at a 20-year low and a calf is ~2 years from the parlour. New plants must run to cover fixed costs, so they push product into the market before supply or demand can rebalance. Plants also ramp gradually (~50% of nameplate early), so cheese output keeps climbing into 2026 as they fill.

06 / 10

Geography

The map is moving to the Plains

Cows and plants are concentrating in the milk-short Central Plains & Southwest, while the milk-long Upper Midwest and contracting California are left balancing surplus. Even as national output fell ~3M lb/day (Dec-24 vs Dec-23), CO/KS/TX/SD together added ~5M lb/day.

Deep dive

The Upper-Midwest negative basis

Wisconsin & Minnesota are structurally milk-long — they make far more milk than local fluid demand, so most goes to manufacturing. With cheese prices soft and new capacity landing elsewhere, the region was left holding surplus milk. Four symptoms follow, and they compound.

Basis, defined

Mailbox = announced Class/blend price + basis. Basis is normally positive (quality, hauling, over-order premiums). In an oversupplied manufacturing region those premiums evaporate and invert — farm milk clears below the FMMO Class III price. That is negative basis.

The depooling doom-loop

When Class III jumps relative to Class IV, the PPD goes negative (blend < Class III). Handlers then depool Class III milk to avoid paying into the pool — which drains pool value and makes the PPD more negative for whoever stays in. The June-2025 make-allowance hike thinned margins further.

08 / 10

The twist nobody priced

Whey didn't glut — it bifurcated

The pre-boom fear was a dry-whey glut. Instead: commodity dry whey is soft, but high-protein WPC80 / WPI are in acute shortage. Extra cheese made plenty of liquid whey — but the bottleneck is fractionation / spray-dry capacity, not cheese vats, and new fractionation won't produce until 2027–28. GLP-1 & the protein craze are pulling demand hard.

09 / 10

The arc

A trough now, a tightening by 2027

This is a cycle, not a permanent glut. Capacity front-loaded the pain; supply and demand catch up on a lag.

2025
Trough wave. Leprino (Lubbock TX), Hilmar (Dodge City KS), Valley Queen (Milbank SD), Great Lakes (Franklinville NY), Darigold (Pasco WA), fairlife (Webster NY) all reach operation — the single biggest step-up. Prices sag.
2026
Fill & secondary wave. 2025 plants ramp from ~50% toward nameplate (output keeps rising); whey-protein plants, Walmart Robinson TX, Idaho Milk Products come on. Surplus window at its widest.
2027
Tightening. Heifer-driven herd rebuild finally arrives; demand & exports catch up; fractionation capacity (Southwest Cheese WPI, Land O'Lakes Tulare) starts relieving the protein squeeze.
2028+
Third build-out. Chobani Rome NY, Daisy Brand Boone IA, Bel/Babybel Brookings SD, fairlife Coopersville MI — a fresh demand-for-milk pulse into the next cycle.

10 / 10

The payoff

How to re-wire your S&D model

The old single-commodity, national, milk-first model will misread this cycle. Eight adjustments:

  • 1Price product-first, derive milk. Model CME cheese/butter/NFDM/whey → apply class formulas (with June-2025 make allowances) → Class III/IV → blend → mailbox. Capacity shocks hit output prices first.
  • 2Split capacity from supply. Two constrained variables, not one. Add a per-plant utilization ramp (~50%→full over 1–2 yrs) so cheese output keeps rising even when milk is flat.
  • 3Balance by component, not fluid volume. Track butterfat and protein/skim-solids separately — that split is what drives the Class III/IV divergence and the whey story.
  • 4Give whey its own sub-model. Dry whey vs WPC80/WPI are different markets; the binding constraint is fractionation capacity (relief in 2027–28), so carry a persistent, wide spread.
  • 5Regionalize. At minimum Upper-Midwest (long, negative-basis risk) vs Plains/SW (short, positive basis, cow migration) vs West (contracting). Basis is now a structural regional variable.
  • 6Make basis, PPD & depooling explicit outputs. Model the Class III–IV spread that triggers negative PPD and depooling, not a fixed basis constant.
  • 7Asymmetric supply lags. Capacity is fast & lumpy; milk is slow (heifers at 20-yr low, ~2-yr lag). Components are the near-term lever; headcount rebuild is a 2027 story.
  • 8Demand = protein trend + export swing. Add GLP-1/high-protein structural demand (esp. whey) and treat exports as the swing absorber — with competition risk (4 of 5 majors growing) and Mexico concentration (~28%).
